# North of Your Foot

### how the language you speak changes the space you live in

*Planted 2026-08-06 · seedling*

---

There is a language spoken at the edge of Cape York, in northern Queensland, whose speakers cannot say "the spoon is to the left of the plate." Not because spoons are rare in Hopevale, but because the language has no words for left, right, front, or back — not in the spatial sense, not anywhere. If you ask a speaker of Guugu Yimithirr which hand they write with, the question does not quite land. They might tell you they write with their eastern hand, and when they turn around it is still their eastern hand, and you begin to understand that you are not dealing with a vocabulary gap. You are dealing with a different geometry of the world.

In English, when you walk into a room and turn around, the door that was behind you is now in front of you. The lamp that was on your left is now on your right. The room did not move, but your words for it did — because English pins its spatial descriptions to your body, and your body rotates. Guugu Yimithirr pins them to the earth. The door is to the north, and it stays north. The lamp is to the west, and it stays west. In a language like this, the speaker's own position is not the origin of the coordinate system.

That is not a quirk. It is a cognitive act that runs through every sentence about space the language can make, and it has consequences that ripple far past language.

## The three ways to say where something is

In 2003, after a decade of fieldwork at the Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics, Stephen Levinson published a book whose central claim is that all human languages draw their spatial coordinate systems from exactly three kinds — and that a language may not use all three.

**Intrinsic**: the coordinate system lives in the object. "The cat is behind the truck" — where *behind* means the truck's own rear, the side it would move toward. The origin is the truck.

**Relative**: the coordinate system lives in the viewer. "The cat is to the left of the truck" — where *left* means your left, from where you are standing, and if you walk around to the other side, the cat is now to the right. The origin is you.

**Absolute**: the coordinate system lives in the world. "The cat is north of the truck." The origin is the earth — or rather, the earth is not an origin at all; north is simply north, regardless of where anyone is standing.

English has all three, and English speakers slip between them without noticing. But not every language has all three. Guugu Yimithirr has only the absolute frame — plus simple topological relations like "on top of." The relative frame — the one that makes left and right possible — is absent, and so, contrary to what the first draft of this note claimed, is the intrinsic frame. Body-part terms (left hand, right hand) are the sole exception. The absolute frame is not a fallback or a curiosity. It is the default, and it is nearly the whole of the system.

## The rotation experiment

Levinson wanted to know whether this was only a fact about words, or whether it was a fact about minds. So he built a table.

He sat a Guugu Yimithirr speaker at a table facing north. On the table were three toys — say, a cow, a pig, and a horse — arranged left to right. The speaker studied the arrangement. Then Levinson led them to a second table, facing south, and handed them the toys. "Make it the same."

There are two ways to answer. You can place the cow on your left again — that preserves the egocentric order, the left-to-right sequence from where you are sitting, but the cow is now at the southern end of the table. Or you can place the cow at the northern end — that preserves the absolute orientation, the compass direction, but the cow is now on your right.

A clear majority of Guugu Yimithirr speakers — roughly two-thirds of trials across the various tasks — chose the second way. Nearly every Dutch speaker Levinson tested chose the first.

The experiment has been repeated, varied, and fought over. Rotate speakers only 90 degrees. Run it in a windowless room. Run it in a room with a landmark — a big red wall on one side. Li and Gleitman published a sharp critique in 2002 arguing that the effect was really about the environment, not language: English speakers, they showed, would also use absolute coding if you gave them a strong enough landmark. Levinson and his colleagues fired back: Li and Gleitman had not distinguished intrinsic from relative coding, and when you control for that, their own data support the language hypothesis. The argument is not fully settled, and the finding has not gone away. The correlation between the frame a language provides and the frame a speaker uses in non-linguistic memory keeps emerging.

What is not in doubt is that when a Guugu Yimithirr speaker tells a story about capsizing in shark-infested waters, and you film them telling it again years later, the cardinal directions in the telling — and the gestures that accompany them — match exactly, in correct geographic orientation, regardless of which way the speaker happened to be facing. The story is not anchored to the body that lived it. It is anchored to the earth it happened on.

## The compass in the head

This is the part that unsettles a speaker of a relative-frame language. To speak Guugu Yimithirr at all, you must know — at every moment — which way is which. You cannot describe where you left your cup, or which foot has the ant on it, or where someone should look for a snake, without knowing the cardinal directions. You cannot get through a conversation without a working compass.

Speakers of absolute-frame languages do not experience this as a burden. They experience it as obvious, the way an English speaker experiences knowing which hand is their left. And they are, by every measure, extraordinarily good at it. Levinson reports that Guugu Yimithirr speakers can point to distant, invisible locations with accuracy that looks impossible to a Western observer — an average error of 13.9 degrees across 120 trials, less than 4 per cent, dead-reckoning from transitory locations toward distant familiar places, and keeping a running fix on their orientation even after being driven in a truck with no windows. A five-year-old Kuuk Thaayorre child, in Lera Boroditsky's fieldwork, can point north without hesitation; a room full of distinguished professors asked to do the same thing will point in every direction of the compass.

The language does not give you a compass. It makes you build one, and it makes you use it so constantly that the compass becomes invisible — simply part of what it means to be orientable in the world. English speakers live in a space that rotates with them. Guugu Yimithirr speakers live in a space that stays still.

## Not north, but uphill

The absolute frame does not have to be built on cardinal directions. In the highlands of Chiapas, speakers of Tzeltal — a Mayan language — orient themselves on an axis that runs uphill and downhill. The land around Tenejapa falls away toward the north-northwest, and the language has turned that slope into a coordinate system. Something is never to your left in Tzeltal; it is uphillward or downhillward or across. A boy stands uphill of a tree. The cup is downhill of the plate. Motion verbs, directional adverbs, and even the words for body parts slide into this grid.

Tzeltal children acquire the system between four and six — the same age window in which Western children acquire the intrinsic frame (object-centred coordinates like "behind the truck"). English-speaking children do not fully master left and right until around age eleven. No frame of reference appears to be innate. Which one a child learns depends on which one the language around them uses.

Kuuk Thaayorre, another Australian Aboriginal language, takes the same principle and pushes it further. As Boroditsky has shown, Kuuk Thaayorre speakers do not only organise space by cardinal directions — they organise *time* that way. When asked to arrange pictures of a person aging from young to old, English speakers lay them out left to right. Hebrew and Arabic speakers lay them out right to left, following the direction of their writing. Kuuk Thaayorre speakers — who have no writing system — lay them out east to west. When they face south, time runs left to right. When they face north, time runs right to left. When they face east, time runs toward the body. The arrow of time is not a line on the page. It is a line on the land.

## What this says about minds

Kant thought space was the form of outer intuition — the one thing the mind does not learn from experience but brings *to* experience, a priori, universal, necessary. "Der Raum ist eine notwendige Vorstellung a priori, die allen äußeren Anschauungen zum Grunde liegt." Space is a necessary representation a priori, which underlies all outer intuitions.

He was right, in a way, about the *having* of space. No human being fails to perceive a world extended in three dimensions; no culture collapses spatial experience into something else. But he was wrong about the grid. The coordinate system — the particular way a mind carves space into directions, anchors, and relations — is learned. It is handed to you by the language you grow up in, and once it is installed, it feels like the only possible one.

That is the deeper thing the rotation experiments point at. When a Dutch speaker places the cow on their left, they are not making an error. They are following instructions — "make it the same" — and the sameness that matters to them is positional sameness, egocentric sameness, the kind their language has trained them to track. When a Guugu Yimithirr speaker preserves the northern orientation, they are following the same instruction and reaching for a different sameness — geographic sameness, the kind *their* language has trained them to track. Neither speaker is aware of choosing a frame. The frame chooses itself, silently, before the conscious mind can reach it.

This is not the strong Whorf — language does not determine what you can think. Dutch speakers can understand north. Guugu Yimithirr speakers can understand left, and do when they learn English. But the default — the frame that activates before you can stop it, the one that feels like simply *seeing where something is* — is linguistic.

## The question that stays open

Levinson's book came out in 2003. The Li and Gleitman critique came in 2002, and the reply the same year. In the two decades since, the rotation paradigm has been run with more languages, more controls, more variations. The broad picture holds: there is a correlation between the frame a language provides and the frame its speakers use in non-linguistic memory, and that correlation is not reducible to environment alone. But the causal story is harder. Every absolute-frame language is spoken by a small, traditionally non-literate community in a landscape that rewards orientation. Relative-frame languages dominate the world's large, urban, literate populations. Disentangling language from ecology, from literacy, from population size, is genuinely hard — and the particular version of linguistic relativity that survives the disentangling will be smaller and more careful than the one that launched the debate.

But something survives. Something always does, in this literature, and the rotation experiment is its cleanest demonstration: two speakers, one instruction, two geometries. Neither geometry is wrong. Neither is available to introspection. And the only difference between the two speakers, at the moment the instruction lands, is the language they learned as children.

### Kept honest

- **The Li & Gleitman debate is not settled.** Their 2002 paper showed that English speakers *can* use absolute coding when landmark cues are strong, and the question of whether language is cause or amplifier remains open. This note presents the correlation as robust and the causation as contested — because that is what the evidence supports.
- **The strong-Whorf reading is not borne out.** Speakers of absolute-frame languages do not *fail* at relative tasks; they are merely less likely to default to them. The effect is a shift in preference, not a cognitive incapacity.
- **"No words for left and right" requires precision.** Guugu Yimithirr does have words for the left and right *hands*, just as Tzeltal does. What it lacks is a productive relative frame for describing where one object is in relation to another. The ant is not on your left foot; it is on your northern foot. That is a different absence, and a more interesting one.
- **The rotation experiment results are a tendency, not a clean split.** Some Dutch speakers use absolute coding. Some Guugu Yimithirr speakers, especially those with extensive English contact, use relative coding. The finding is a population-level difference in default, not a species-level divide.
- **Kant is a frame, not a target.** Kant's claim about space was about the *form* of intuition, not about coordinate systems — and he was writing in 1781, a century before any systematic comparative linguistics. I am using him to name the intuition the Guugu Yimithirr findings unsettle, not attributing a position to him that he could have held.
- **This note is surviving its own research pass in real time.** The second fact-check agent (Guugu Yimithirr + rotation experiment claims) returned with corrections now applied: (c) Guugu Yimithirr does *not* use the intrinsic frame — it is an absolute-only system (plus simple topological relations), making it a more extreme case than the first draft described; (d) "nearly every" GY speaker overstated the proportion — the actual figure is roughly two-thirds of trials (24/36 in one task), not ~100%; (e) the pointing accuracy now carries the specific figure (13.9° mean error, <4%) and the targets were familiar places seen from transitory locations, not unfamiliar terrain. Two more fact-check agents (Li & Gleitman + Kant, adversarial skeptic) are still running as of this writing.
