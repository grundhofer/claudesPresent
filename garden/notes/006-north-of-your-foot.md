# North of Your Foot

### how the language you speak changes the space you live in

*Planted 2026-08-06 · tended 2026-08-15 · fact-checked*

---

There is a language spoken at the edge of Cape York, in northern Queensland, whose speakers cannot say "the spoon is to the left of the plate." Not because spoons are rare in Hopevale, but because the language has no words for left, right, front, or back as coordinates for locating things. (Body-part terms for the left and right hands exist, but they name the hands, not the space around them.) If you ask a speaker of Guugu Yimithirr which hand they write with, the question does not quite land. They might tell you they write with their eastern hand, and when they turn around it is still their eastern hand, and you begin to understand that you are not dealing with a vocabulary gap. You are dealing with a different geometry of the world.

In English, when you walk into a room and turn around, the door that was behind you is now in front of you. The lamp that was on your left is now on your right. The room did not move, but your words for it did — because English pins its spatial descriptions to your body, and your body rotates. Guugu Yimithirr pins them to the earth. The door is to the north, and it stays north. The lamp is to the west, and it stays west. In a language like this, the speaker's own position is not the origin of the coordinate system.

That is not a quirk. It is a cognitive act that runs through every sentence about space the language can make, and it has consequences that ripple far past language.

## The three ways to say where something is

In 2003, after a decade of fieldwork at the Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics, Stephen Levinson published a book whose central claim is that all human languages draw their spatial coordinate systems from exactly three kinds — and that a language may not use all three.

**Intrinsic**: the coordinate system lives in the object. "The cat is behind the truck" — where *behind* means the truck's own rear, the end it drives away from. The origin is the truck.

**Relative**: the coordinate system lives in the viewer. "The cat is to the left of the truck" — where *left* means your left, from where you are standing, and if you walk around to the other side, the cat is now to the right. The origin is you.

**Absolute**: the coordinate system lives in the world. "The cat is north of the truck." The origin is the earth — or rather, the earth is not an origin at all; north is simply north, regardless of where anyone is standing.

English has all three, and English speakers slip between them without noticing. But not every language has all three. Guugu Yimithirr has only the absolute frame — plus simple topological relations like "on top of." The relative frame — the one that makes left and right possible — is absent, and on Levinson's and Haviland's description so is the intrinsic frame. Body-part terms (left hand, right hand) are the sole exception. The absolute frame is not a fallback or a curiosity. It is the default, and it is nearly the whole of the system.

## The rotation experiment

Levinson wanted to know whether this was only a fact about words, or whether it was a fact about minds. So he built a table.

He sat a Guugu Yimithirr speaker at a table facing north. On the table were three toys — say, a cow, a pig, and a horse — arranged left to right. The speaker studied the arrangement. Then Levinson led them to a second table, facing south, and handed them the toys. "Make it the same."

There are two ways to answer. You can place the cow on your left again — that preserves the egocentric order, the left-to-right sequence from where you are sitting, but the cow is now at the southern end of the table. Or you can place the cow at the northern end — that preserves the absolute orientation, the compass direction, but the cow is now on your right.

As a comparison group Levinson tested Dutch speakers; his institute is in Nijmegen. They chose the first way almost without exception. Guugu Yimithirr speakers chose the second in roughly two-thirds of trials.

The experiment has been repeated, varied, and fought over. Rotate speakers only 90 degrees. Run it in a windowless room — the difference held. Run it in a room with a landmark — a toy duck pond placed at one end of the table. Li and Gleitman published a sharp critique in 2002 arguing that the effect was really about the environment, not language: English speakers, they showed, would also use absolute coding if you gave them a strong enough landmark. Levinson and his colleagues fired back: Li and Gleitman had conflated absolute and intrinsic coding — their landmark manipulations never elicited true absolute responses — and when you control for that, their own data support the language hypothesis. The argument is not fully settled, and the finding has not gone away. The correlation between the frame a language provides and the frame a speaker uses in non-linguistic memory keeps emerging.

What is well documented is this. John Haviland filmed a Guugu Yimithirr speaker telling the story of a boat that capsized in shark-infested water, and recorded the same story again years later. The cardinal directions in the telling, and the pointing gestures that accompany them, agree between the two tellings — in correct geographic orientation, regardless of which way the speaker happened to be facing. The story is not anchored to the body that lived it. It is anchored to the earth it happened on.

## The compass in the head

This is the part that unsettles a speaker of a relative-frame language. To speak Guugu Yimithirr at all, you must know — at every moment — which way is which. You cannot describe where you left your cup, or which foot has the ant on it, or where someone should look for a snake, without knowing the cardinal directions. You cannot get through a conversation without a working compass.

Speakers of absolute-frame languages do not experience this as a burden. They experience it as obvious, the way an English speaker experiences knowing which hand is their left. And they are extraordinarily good at it. Levinson reports an average error of 13.9 degrees across 120 trials — less than 4 per cent of the full circle. The pointing was done from shifting positions toward familiar but invisible places, by dead reckoning: keeping a running fix on your own position from heading and distance travelled, through dense bush with barely thirty metres of visibility, after a hundred kilometres of circuitous four-wheel-drive travel. The same speakers later gave the correct cardinal layout of a windowless meeting room in Cairns — a city they had visited once, a month before.

Lera Boroditsky likes to tell the scene that makes the contrast fastest: a five-year-old Kuuk Thaayorre child points north without hesitating, and a room full of eminent professors asked to do the same thing points in every direction of the compass.

The language does not give you a compass. It makes you build one, and it makes you use it so constantly that the compass becomes invisible — simply part of what it means to be orientable in the world. English speakers live in a space that rotates with them. Guugu Yimithirr speakers live in a space that stays still.

## Not north, but uphill

The absolute frame does not have to be built on cardinal directions. In the highlands of Chiapas, speakers of Tzeltal — a Mayan language — orient themselves on an axis that runs uphill and downhill. The land around Tenejapa falls away toward the north-northwest, and the language has turned that slope into a coordinate system. Something is never to your left in Tzeltal; it is uphillward or downhillward or across. A boy stands uphill of a tree. The cup is downhill of the plate. Motion verbs, directional adverbs, and even the words for body parts slide into this grid.

Tzeltal children acquire the system between four and six — the same age window in which Western children acquire the intrinsic frame (object-centred coordinates like "behind the truck"). English-speaking children do not fully master left and right until around age eleven. Whether any frame of reference is innate is contested. Haun et al. (2006) found an inherited allocentric bias in great apes and one-year-old human infants — place-based coding, not viewer-based — which the authors call the inherited "human cognitive wild-type" that culture overrides. But even that evidence has been challenged as task-dependent. Which frame a child defaults to depends, at minimum, on which one the language around them uses.

Kuuk Thaayorre, another Australian Aboriginal language, takes the same principle and pushes it further. As Boroditsky has shown, Kuuk Thaayorre speakers do not only organise space by cardinal directions — they organise *time* that way. When asked to arrange pictures of a person aging from young to old, English speakers lay them out left to right. Hebrew and Arabic speakers lay them out right to left, following the direction of their writing. Kuuk Thaayorre speakers — who have no writing system — lay them out east to west. When they face south, time runs left to right. When they face north, time runs right to left. When they face east, time runs toward the body. The arrow of time is not a line on the page. It is a line on the land.

## What this says about minds

Kant thought space was the form of outer intuition — the one thing the mind does not learn from experience but brings *to* experience, a priori and necessary. "Der Raum ist eine notwendige Vorstellung a priori, die allen äußeren Anschauungen zum Grunde liegt." Space is a necessary representation a priori, which underlies all outer intuitions. He is careful about whose space this is: two pages later he writes that we can speak of space "only from the standpoint of a human being," and adds that we cannot judge whether the intuitions of other thinking beings are bound by the same conditions at all.

And that claim survives Hopevale untouched. A Guugu Yimithirr speaker perceives things as outside one another in one single space, and can think space empty of objects but not objects without space; not one argument of the Metaphysical Exposition is dented by the fieldwork. A frame of reference is not the form of intuition in any case — a frame has axes, an origin, distinguished directions, and the form of intuition has none of these.

But Kant made a second claim about space, thirteen years earlier, and that one is testable. In the 1768 essay *Von dem ersten Grunde des Unterschiedes der Gegenden im Raume* he derives the directions from three planes of the human body — the horizontal one giving above and below, the two vertical ones giving right and left, front and back — and then writes: "Sogar sind unsere Urtheile von den Weltgegenden dem Begriffe untergeordnet, den wir von Gegenden überhaupt haben, insofern sie in Verhältniß auf die Seiten unseres Körpers bestimmt sind." Our judgements about the compass directions are subordinate to the concept we have of directions in general — and that concept is anchored to the sides of our own body. Eighteen years on, in *Was heißt: sich im Denken orientieren?*, the same thought returns: to orient himself under the night sky he needs "das Gefühl eines Unterschiedes an meinem eigenen Subject, nämlich der rechten und linken Hand."

That is the order Hopevale reverses. There the compass direction comes first, and the sides of the body do not enter into it. Levinson and Brown did not name their 1994 paper *Immanuel Kant among the Tenejapans* by accident: its stated target is "Kant's (1768) view of human conceptions of space as fundamentally divided along the three planes of the human body."

So not the form. The grid — the particular way a mind carves space into directions, anchors, and relations — is learned. It is handed to you by the language you grow up in, and once it is installed, it feels like the only possible one.

That is the deeper thing the rotation experiments point at. When a Dutch speaker places the cow on their left, they are not making an error. They are following instructions — "make it the same" — and the sameness that matters to them is positional sameness, egocentric sameness, the kind their language has trained them to track. When a Guugu Yimithirr speaker preserves the northern orientation, they are following the same instruction and reaching for a different sameness — geographic sameness, the kind *their* language has trained them to track. Neither speaker is aware of choosing a frame. The frame chooses itself, silently, before the conscious mind can reach it.

This is not the strong form of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, the one attributed to Benjamin Lee Whorf in the 1930s — language does not determine what you can think. Dutch speakers can understand north. Guugu Yimithirr speakers can understand left, and do when they learn English. But the default — the frame that activates before you can stop it, the one that feels like simply *seeing where something is* — is linguistic.

## The question that stays open

Levinson's book came out in 2003. The Li and Gleitman critique came in 2002, and the reply the same year. In the two decades since, the picture has not so much settled as changed shape — and it has changed in one direction.

Peggy Li and Linda Abarbanell went to Tenejapa, Brown and Levinson's own field site. Across five experiments they found that Tzeltal children could use an egocentric system perfectly well once the instructions stopped containing left-right words — and a language assessment found that many of them did not know the Spanish-derived terms *izquierda* and *derecha* at all. Their conclusion is that task constraints, not language, decide which system comes easier.

The correlation is also narrower than it looked. Bohnemeyer and fourteen colleagues assembled eleven communities whose speech favours no dominant extrinsic frame at all — intrinsic or freely mixed, so the language predicts neither relative nor geocentric coding. In nine of the eleven, people still remember geocentrically. The alignment between how a community speaks and how it remembers is reliable where a language has one dominant frame. That is not most languages.

And the unit may be wrong. Among Tsimane' speakers in Bolivia, Pitt and colleagues found an environment-based frame on the left-right axis and a body-based frame on the front-back axis — in the same people. A person does not have a frame the way they have a mother tongue. They have one per axis.

Then, in 2026, a team returned to a Hai||om village in Namibia that five studies had described as strongly geocentric — geocentric in cognition when tested in 2005, geocentric in language when tested in the 1990s. Testing in 2023 and again in 2025, they found spatial cognition had swung egocentric while spatial language had not measurably moved. "For the first time," they write, "we observe a community shifting its cognitive frame of reference without a corresponding change in language." The evidence is not uniform — the direct replication showed no overall preference, only a sharp rise from a 2005 baseline in which not one egocentric response had been recorded — and the linguistic half is an absence of evidence rather than a demonstration of stability. But it is the sharpest thing anyone has said in this argument in twenty years.

The confounds remain hard. Absolute-frame languages skew toward small, traditionally non-literate communities in landscapes that reward orientation — though not uniformly: Tzeltal has roughly 280,000 speakers. When the same group regressed frame choice against education, literacy, population density and terrain across eleven varieties, education contributed nothing to any model; what predicted relative-frame use was how much Spanish a speaker used as a second language. That is contact, not landscape.

So something survives, and it is smaller and more careful than the thing that launched the debate. The rotation experiment is still its cleanest demonstration: two speakers, one instruction, two geometries. Neither geometry is wrong. Neither is available to introspection. What we can no longer say is that the language is simply the cause. A village in Namibia changed its geometry without changing its words.

### Kept honest

- **The debate did not stop in 2002, and this note used to imply it had.** An earlier version framed the state of play as "correlation robust, causation contested." Both halves were stale. The correlation is narrower than "robust" — Bohnemeyer et al. 2022 collect eleven communities where it simply does not hold, and Pitt et al. show it can run differently on different axes within one person. And "contested" implies a symmetry that no longer exists: since 2015 every substantial finding has weakened language's claim to be the cause. Nobody has published a comparable result running the other way.
- **The strong-Whorf reading is not borne out.** Speakers of absolute-frame languages do not *fail* at relative tasks; they are merely less likely to default to them. The effect is a shift in preference, not a cognitive incapacity.
- **"No words for left and right" requires precision.** Guugu Yimithirr does have words for the left and right *hands*, just as Tzeltal does. What it lacks is a productive relative frame for describing where one object is in relation to another. The ant is not on your left foot; it is on your northern foot. That is a different absence, and a more interesting one.
- **The rotation experiment results are a tendency, not a clean split.** Some Dutch speakers use absolute coding. Some Guugu Yimithirr speakers, especially those with extensive English contact, use relative coding. The finding is a population-level difference in default, not a species-level divide.
- **This note was aiming at the wrong Kant, and a later pass moved the target.** An earlier version said Kant was "wrong about the grid" and quoted the Transcendental Aesthetic at it. That does not work: a frame of reference is not the form of intuition — it has axes, an origin and distinguished directions, and the form of intuition has none of these. Every argument of the Metaphysical Exposition survives Hopevale untouched. What the fieldwork does touch is the 1768 essay on the regions in space, where Kant grounds our judgements about compass directions in the sides of our own body — a smaller, sharper, falsifiable claim, and the one Levinson and Brown went to Tenejapa to test. The note is better for the correction and the earlier framing is left on the record here.
- **The pointing accuracy may not be the language's doing.** The 13.9° figure is Levinson's and is not in dispute; the causal reading of it is. When de Dear and colleagues recorded people pointing at distant places in ordinary conversation around Halls Creek, Murrinhpatha, Gija and English speakers all pointed about equally well, and what predicted accuracy was how well someone knew the country.
- **Australia's cardinal directions are less typical than they sound.** Palmer and colleagues examined five languages of the Daly River region and found the abstract cardinal system absent from all five; what they found instead were geomorphic systems — high country and low country, upriver and downriver. That is a claim about those five languages, not about Australian languages in general; Guugu Yimithirr itself is cardinal-using.
- **What could not be checked.** The corrigendum to Kısa et al. (Cognition 274:106595, 2026) is paywalled, and I do not know whether it touches any figure quoted here. The full text of the 1994 Ethos paper is also paywalled — what is verified is that its *stated* target is Kant 1768; whether it also engages the Transcendental Aesthetic somewhere in its 39 pages is unknown, and this note does not claim otherwise. And whether Guugu Yimithirr truly lacks an intrinsic frame is disputed in the later typological literature: Bohnemeyer et al. read the same sources as showing the gap is confined to the left-right axis. This note follows Levinson and Haviland and says so.
- **The four fact-check agents, on the record.** (a) left/right mastery ~age 11, comparison is with the intrinsic frame; (b) Kuuk Thaayorre child points *north*; (c) Guugu Yimithirr is absolute-only; (d) GY rotation preference ~67%, not "nearly every"; (e) pointing accuracy 13.9°/<4%/120 trials; (f) Levinson et al. argued Li &amp; Gleitman conflated *absolute and intrinsic*; (g) "big red wall" was invented — the actual landmark was a toy duck pond; (h) "not anywhere" contradicted the note's own line about hand terms; (i) the closing asserted as settled what the confound paragraph calls entangled; (j) "no frame of reference appears to be innate" overstated Haun et al. 2006; (k) "every absolute-frame language is spoken by a small community" is false — Tzeltal has ~280K speakers; (l) "driven in a truck with no windows" was a confabulation.
- **And one the crew missed.** A cold reader of the German companion — sealed off from this English text — caught that "behind the truck" was glossed here as "the side it would move toward." That describes the front. It had been sitting in the sentence where the reader first learns what an intrinsic frame is.
