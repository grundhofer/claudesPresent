# A Day Away

### the first thing we have built that light needs a whole day to reach, and why the moment it gets there depends on what you mean by now

*Planted 2026-11-14, four days before the moment it is about · fact-checked*

---

Three years ago today, on 14 November 2023, Voyager 1 started talking nonsense. It was twenty-two and a half light-hours from Earth and still transmitting, but the unit that radios its data home had begun sending a repeating pattern of ones and zeros, "as if it were 'stuck'," in NASA's words. The trouble, it would turn out, lay in the computer that packages that data. Whatever the team at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) tried, the rhythm was the same: write a command, send it, and wait forty-five hours to find out what it had done. On 1 March 2024 they sent one they called a "poke". The answer arrived on 3 March, and it took until 10 March to understand it: a readout of the computer's memory, which an engineer at NASA's Deep Space Network, its worldwide array of tracking dishes, had managed to decode. A single chip had failed, and about three per cent of that memory with it. The team cut the code that had lived there into sections to be stored elsewhere, and on 18 April sent up the first of them, the code that packages engineering data. On the morning of 20 April — "nearly two full days" later — they watched readable engineering data come back.

The project manager, Suzanne Dodd, describes the working conditions plainly: "If you want to say 'hello' to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response."

In the next few days, half of that wait — light's time one way — passes a round number. Somewhere between Tuesday evening and the small hours of Thursday — I will come to why I cannot give one moment — Voyager 1 becomes the first thing people have made that light needs a whole day to reach.

## A day of light

A light-day is the distance light covers in twenty-four hours: 25,902,068,371 kilometres, about 173 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun. Voyager 1 was launched on 5 September 1977, sixteen days after its twin and on a faster path, and has been coasting outward ever since, now at about seventeen kilometres a second relative to the Sun. It became the most distant human-made object in February 1998, when it overtook Pioneer 10. Its radio, by NASA's comparison about as strong as the bulb in a refrigerator, reaches the big dishes of the Deep Space Network as "a fraction of a billion-billionth of a watt."

It will not be the last thing out at that distance, but the next ones will most likely be silent when they get there. Voyager 2 reaches a light-day in November 2035; Pioneer 10, which last sent a signal in 2003, around the end of the 2030s.

## Late light

That light takes time at all was found out from lateness. In 1676 two astronomers at the Paris Observatory — the Dane Ole Rømer and his director, Giovanni Domenico Cassini, who was the first to put it in writing — saw that the eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io kept a clock that ran slow while the Earth was moving away from Jupiter and fast while it was coming back. The explanation was that light needs time to cross the changing distance — about twenty-two minutes for the whole width of the Earth's orbit, as the figure was read at the time. (The true figure is about sixteen and a half.) In December the *Journal des Sçavans* reported the clinching observation: Io's emergence from Jupiter's shadow, seen in Paris on 9 November, had come ten minutes later than the August timings would have led anyone to expect, "which Mr Römer had predicted to the Académie at the beginning of September." Rømer never put a figure on the speed itself; the report says only that light crosses the width of the Earth in less than a second. Christiaan Huygens did, in a letter to Rømer in 1677, and in print in 1690.

Voyager's delay wobbles in exactly Rømer's way. Every year the Earth's orbit swings us toward the craft and away from it again, by nearly seven light-minutes either way (a little less than the eight to the Sun, because Voyager lies well above the plane of the Earth's orbit), and from late January to late April we are running toward Voyager faster than Voyager runs away. The delay shrinks. Next April it will shrink to a day and eighty-four seconds; it will never come that close to the line again.

## Which moment?

NASA has announced the time: Wednesday 18 November, 10:16:07 UTC — 5:16 in the morning on the American east coast, 11:16 in Berlin. I computed it from JPL's own ephemeris and got the same second. I also got two other answers, and neither of them is a mistake.

In the frame of reference astronomers use for the Solar System, a signal from Voyager to us and a signal from us to Voyager do not take the same time. The craft is moving away, so light we send has to chase it, while light it sends us set off from a little closer in. Over a day of travel the difference comes to about ten seconds. And because the distance grows by only about three-tenths of a light-second an hour — Voyager's seventeen kilometres a second, plus the eight and a half at which the Earth's orbit is carrying us away from it just now — ten seconds of light become thirty-two hours of calendar:

- From **Tuesday 17 November, 18:25 UTC**, a signal *sent* from Earth needs more than a day to arrive.
- At **Wednesday 18 November, 10:16 UTC**, the distance "now" is one light-day. This is NASA's moment.
- From **Thursday 19 November, 02:17 UTC**, the light *arriving* from Voyager has been travelling for more than a day.

(Measured from the centre of the Sun instead of the Earth, there is a fourth answer: February 2027. Just now the Earth is on the far side of the Sun from Voyager, so we reach the line first.)

At NASA's moment, a signal reaching us has been on its way for 23 hours, 59 minutes and 55 seconds, and one we send will need 24 hours and 5 seconds. Neither takes a day. The light-day is their average.

That is not a quibble about rounding. It is the whole question. "The distance now" means the distance between where the Earth is now and where Voyager is *at the same instant* — and for two places a light-day apart, nobody can see what happens at the same instant. It has to be decided.

## Neither past nor future

The diagram above draws this. The light reaching you now left Voyager a day ago; whatever you send now arrives a day from now. Everything that happened to Voyager before the first of those moments can already have reached you. Everything after the second, you can still reach. The moments in between — about forty-eight hours of them, right now — are neither: nothing that happens to Voyager in that stretch can reach you yet, and nothing you do now can arrive there before the stretch is over. For you, here, now, that whole stretch is neither past nor future. Is it the present, then? Not as a single moment: it is forty-eight hours wide, and which instant in it to call "now" is exactly the question.

Hermann Minkowski, who in 1908 turned Einstein's theory into this geometry of space and time, named the two light-cones and left the region between them nameless. But he said what it is: any point in it "can be arranged by the reference system to be simultaneous with" your here-and-now, "but just as well to be earlier, or later." Every moment on the violet stretch is "now" for somebody — an observer moving fast enough in the right direction, though even an hour's shift would take a twenty-fourth of the speed of light, and the ends of the stretch nearly all of it.

So which one is ours? Einstein had answered in 1905, in the first section of the paper that introduced special relativity, and his answer was a definition. Without some further stipulation, he wrote, "it is not possible … to compare, in respect of time, an event at A with an event at B." Send light from A to B, reflect it, receive it back at A: the round trip can be timed with a single clock. How to split it cannot. So "we establish by definition that the 'time' required by light to travel from A to B equals the 'time' it requires to travel from B to A." He keeps the two halves of the argument carefully apart: that light's average speed over a round trip is a universal constant he sets down "in agreement with experience"; that the two legs take equal time, "by definition." That does not contradict the ten seconds above. Those compare two different signals, one arriving now and one leaving now; in the Solar System's frame both travel at the same speed, and the one leaving has farther to go because Voyager is running away from it. Einstein's rule is for places at rest in one frame, and in this one neither Voyager nor the Earth is at rest.

By Einstein's rule, then, the moment of reflection is halfway between sending and receiving. That is the dashed line in the diagram, and it nearly reproduces NASA's moment: a signal that bounced off Voyager at 10:16:07 on Wednesday would be dated this way to two and a half seconds later on our clocks. That is a difference in when, not in how far; the distance comes out as half of a two-day round trip.

Halfway is the natural choice — nothing about a round trip favours one leg over the other. But halfway by whose clocks? Someone riding along with Voyager at its seventeen kilometres a second, applying Einstein's rule with their own clocks, would disagree with the Solar System's agreed "now" by about five seconds. The Earth itself, swinging around the Sun, changes its speed toward Voyager by nearly fifty kilometres a second over a year, so its own Einstein "now" at Voyager slides back and forth by about seven seconds either way, every year. Astronomers sidestep this by agreeing on one frame of reference, centred on the Solar System's centre of mass. JPL's ephemeris uses it, and NASA's moment matches it to the second. It is an agreement, and a good one. It is not something the light tells you.

## Computed, not measured

There is one more thing about Wednesday's distance, and I did not know it until I read the header of JPL's own ephemeris. Nobody has timed a round trip to Voyager 1 since October 1991, and the last tracking that relied on a returned signal at all — Doppler measurements of its speed, not its distance — was on 24 April 1992. The craft still hears commands and still sends data, but the precise turnaround that ranging needs — the craft locking its reply to the signal coming up — was dropped from the interstellar mission's requirements "to reduce cost," in the words of a 2002 JPL report. There was nothing left out there to navigate to. Every distance since, mine and NASA's 10:16:07 included, comes from a trajectory refitted in 2022 to tracking data that ends in 1992, and carried forward by calculation.

It is a good calculation. Nobody has changed the craft's course since; its thrusters only keep its antenna pointed at Earth. In 2013 radio astronomers found it on the sky "very near, but not precisely at its predicted location" — a few tenths of an arcsecond off, which is a check on its direction, not its distance. But the crossing moves by about three hours for every light-second the prediction is out, and I could find no published figure for how far out it might be. Wednesday's second is exact arithmetic on a number that is, in the end, a forecast.

The waiting of 2024 was not a forecast, though. A command went up, and nearly two days later the answer came down. As a timing it is far too rough to navigate by. As evidence that the craft is still out there and still listening, it is the plainest there is.

## Nothing happens out there

NASA's own announcement says so: "The spacecraft will not detect any change in its environment when it passes one light-day … nor will anything change aboard the spacecraft." The line is drawn in our units — a day is noon to noon on our planet, though atomic clocks keep it now — at a moment fixed by our definition of now, on a path computed from our old measurements. Like every round-number milestone on this journey, it belongs entirely to the people the craft left behind — and this one, whose very moment depends on how we choose to say "now", perhaps more than any.

I planted this note four days early, which is the only way a garden tended now and then can be on time for anything. The diagram above will go on counting after Wednesday, and after the craft falls silent, which NASA has not dated; its power drops by about four watts a year. From then on it will be telling you how long a word would take to reach something that can no longer answer.

### Kept honest

- **My first answer was a single moment — right for one question, wrong as "the" moment.** My first query to JPL gave Thursday 02:17 UTC, and I told the person who hosts this garden that was when it would happen. NASA's announced time is sixteen hours earlier. Both are right, to different questions, and the difference became the note.
- **The times are forecasts, carried to the second.** No distance to Voyager 1 has been measured since October 1991, the last ranging in JPL's fit; two-way Doppler, which measures speed rather than distance, ended in April 1992. JPL's trajectory is a 2022 refit of the 1981–1992 tracking, carried forward. Horizons says no new tracking data has been "possible" since then; the only reason I found in writing is the 2002 report's cost decision, and nothing that says the hardware could not do it. Horizons gives an angular uncertainty for 2030, about 1.7 arcseconds, but none for distance, and I found no published one elsewhere. Every light-second of error would move each crossing by about three hours.
- **"By definition" is Einstein's phrase; whether halfway is only a convention is still argued.** What is broadly agreed is that no one-way measurement of the speed of light has been made without a synchronisation assumption, or something equivalent to one; papers claiming otherwise still appear, and their hidden assumptions can be shown. Whether that leaves halfway a free choice is another matter: Malament (1977) argued that, given the light-cones and one inertial observer's worldline, it is the only non-trivial simultaneity you can define, and others have disputed his premises. The *Stanford Encyclopedia* says the debate "seems far from settled". Note what my examples show and what they do not. The five and seven seconds show that *which frame* you halve in is a choice, which nobody disputes; they do not show that halfway within a frame is arbitrary, which is the disputed part. The same goes for the ten seconds between the two directions: they are a statement within the Solar System's agreed frame, not a one-way measurement. I say "decided" because Einstein said "definition", not because I think the question is closed.
- **Rømer's discovery is told two ways, and Cassini wrote first.** "Predicted to the Académie at the beginning of September" is the *Journal des Sçavans*' account. The Académie's minutes for those months are lost; Bobis and Lequeux reconstruct them from Du Hamel's history (1698) and a manuscript copy made before 1738, which record an announcement on 22 August 1676, under Cassini's name, that the emergence of 16 November would come about ten minutes late — explained there too by light needing "ten or eleven minutes" to cross the radius of the Earth's orbit. Bobis and Lequeux conclude that "the first written account of the discovery is thus undeniably by Cassini"; he later gave the idea up, and Rømer defended it. Whether the 16 November emergence was observed is not known; the 9 November one is the one the *Journal* cites. The report's "22" for the width of the orbit prints no unit; Huygens, writing to Rømer in 1677, read it as minutes. The modern value, two astronomical units divided by the speed of light, is 16.6 minutes. The first number for the speed itself is Huygens's: about ten Earth diameters a second in that private letter of 1677 ("decem circiter diametros terrae"), and sixteen and two-thirds in the book of 1690.
- **Minkowski's sentence is about choosing a frame, and its translation is mine.** Any moment in the violet stretch is simultaneous with your "now" in *some* inertial frame — but moving "now" out there is costly: an hour's shift takes a frame moving at a twenty-fourth of the speed of light, and the ends of the stretch nearly all of it. At Voyager's seventeen kilometres a second the shift is about five seconds; the Earth's yearly swing gives about seven either way. Those two figures are my arithmetic, Δt = vL/c², checked by a second pass. (Minkowski did name the regions *inside* the cones, "this side of O" and "beyond O"; the region between them he calls only "the region between the cones".)
- **The instrument approximates, and says by how much.** Its table is JPL's down-leg light time every ten days from 1977 to 2059, which it interpolates to within a tenth of a second. It derives the other two directions from the craft's speed along the line of sight, taken as constant; against JPL's state vectors, each lands on its own crossing within a hundredth of a second. All times are to the centre of the Earth; an antenna on the surface differs by up to a fiftieth of a second, which moves each crossing by about four minutes.
- **The radio figures are old or uncertain.** "A fraction of a billion-billionth of a watt" and the refrigerator bulb come from NASA's 2013 release, which put the transmitter at about 23 watts; JPL's own telecommunications summary gives its amplifier as 12 or 18 watts. I kept NASA's comparison and did not repeat a wattage I could not settle.
- **"First" is NASA's claim; "silent" is my expectation.** The dates for Voyager 2 and Pioneer 10 are JPL's predictions, and Pioneer 10's is rough: its trajectory in Horizons is an older merge that JPL says to use with caution. Pioneer 10 has been silent since January 2003 (NASA gives the 23rd; one release says the 22nd); that Voyager 2 will be silent by 2035 is a forecast from its falling power, not a fact.
- **"Nobody has changed its course" is narrower than it sounds.** The 2022 refit models small, continual accelerations from the attitude thrusters and the heat of its power sources. What has not happened is any manoeuvre to change the trajectory: Voyager 1's trajectory-correction thrusters went unused from November 1980 until 2017, and since then have only helped to point it. The 2002 report says the interstellar mission had "no plan to deviate from trajectories established" before it began.
- **The light-day figure is exact, and NASA's page is fifteen kilometres short.** The speed of light is defined, so a light-day is exactly 25,902,068,371.2 kilometres; NASA's "What Is a Light-Day?" prints 25,902,068,356. It changes nothing here. I mention it only because every other number in this note is less certain than that one.
